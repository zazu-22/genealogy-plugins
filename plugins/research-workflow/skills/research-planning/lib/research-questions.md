# Research Questions

Formulating effective research questions that guide productive research.

## Why Questions Matter

### The Problem with "Research Everything"

Without specific questions:
- Research is unfocused and inefficient
- You don't know when you're done
- You collect information without purpose
- You miss important connections

### The Power of Good Questions

With specific questions:
- Research has clear direction
- Success is measurable
- Sources are selected purposefully
- Conclusions are possible

## The SMART Framework

### Specific

**Weak**: "Find out about John Smith"
**Strong**: "Determine John Smith's date of death"

Ask: What exactly do I want to know?

### Measurable

**Weak**: "Learn more about the family"
**Strong**: "Identify the children of William and Mary Jones"

Ask: How will I know when I've answered it?

### Achievable

**Weak**: "Find the original immigrant" (for 1600s family)
**Strong**: "Identify the parents of John Smith (1850-1920)"

Ask: Is evidence likely to exist?

### Relevant

**Weak**: "Research all Smiths in Ohio"
**Strong**: "Determine if John Smith of Columbus is related to my John Smith of Zanesville"

Ask: Does this advance my research goals?

### Time-bound

**Weak**: "Research the Smith family"
**Strong**: "Identify John Smith's residence from 1870 to 1890"

Ask: What's the scope/time period?

## Question Types

### Identity Questions

"Is this person who I think they are?"

**Examples**:
- Is John Smith in the 1850 census the same John Smith in 1860?
- Is Mary Jones who married Thomas Smith the same Mary Jones in this probate?
- Are there multiple William Johnsons in this county?

**Key sources**: Census sequences, vital records, consistent details

### Relationship Questions

"How are these people connected?"

**Examples**:
- Who were Mary's parents?
- Was James the son or nephew of William?
- What was the relationship between these two John Smiths?

**Key sources**: Vital records, census, probate, DNA

### Event Questions

"What happened and when/where?"

**Examples**:
- When did John die?
- Where were John and Mary married?
- When did the family move from Kentucky to Ohio?

**Key sources**: Vital records, church records, newspaper, land records

### Origin Questions

"Where did they come from?"

**Examples**:
- Where was John born?
- When did the family immigrate?
- What country did the family come from?

**Key sources**: Census, naturalization, church records, passenger lists

### Completeness Questions

"Is my picture complete?"

**Examples**:
- Do I have all the children of this couple?
- Have I found all the marriages?
- Are there other branches of this family?

**Key sources**: Comprehensive record searches, cluster research

## From Vague to Specific

### Transformation Examples

**Vague**: "Research my great-grandmother"
**First refinement**: "Research Sarah Jones, born circa 1870"
**Specific**: "Identify the parents of Sarah Jones, who married William Smith in Ohio in 1895"

**Vague**: "Find out when the family came to America"
**First refinement**: "Determine when the Schmidt family immigrated"
**Specific**: "Identify when Johann Schmidt (1820-1890) arrived in the United States and from what port"

**Vague**: "Learn about the Civil War veteran"
**First refinement**: "Research James Wilson's Civil War service"
**Specific**: "Determine James Wilson's regiment, service dates, and any pension application"

## Building a Research Plan

### From Question to Plan

1. **State the question clearly**
   - Write it down exactly

2. **Identify what you already know**
   - List known facts
   - List sources already consulted

3. **Identify what sources might answer it**
   - Primary sources likely to have information
   - Secondary sources for leads

4. **Prioritize sources**
   - Most likely to answer question
   - Most accessible
   - Most reliable

5. **Execute systematically**
   - Search each source
   - Document results (positive and negative)

6. **Evaluate and iterate**
   - Did you answer the question?
   - Do you need to refine the question?
   - Do you have new questions?

### Planning Template

```
RESEARCH QUESTION:
[Specific, clear question]

WHAT I KNOW:
- [Fact 1]
- [Fact 2]
- [Sources already consulted]

- [ ] [Source 1]

- [ ] [Source 3]
- [ ] [Source 4]

- [ ] [Source 5]

[To be filled in as research progresses]
```

## Managing Multiple Questions

### The Question Queue

Maintain a list of research questions:
1. Current focus (1-3 questions)
2. Next up (questions to tackle soon)
3. Backlog (questions for later)
4. Blocked (waiting for something)

### Prioritization Criteria

| Factor | Consider |
|--------|----------|
| Impact | Does answering this unlock other questions? |
| Feasibility | How likely is evidence available? |
| Urgency | Is there a time-sensitive opportunity? |
| Interest | Personal motivation matters |

### When to Move On

Move to next question when:
- Question is answered satisfactorily
- Available sources are exhausted
- You're blocked waiting for records
- Diminishing returns on effort

## Common Pitfalls

### Too Broad

**Problem**: "Who was my ancestor's family?"
**Issue**: Undefined scope, no clear endpoint
**Fix**: Break into specific sub-questions

### Too Narrow

**Problem**: "What was John's exact street address in 1872?"
**Issue**: May not be answerable, doesn't advance research
**Fix**: Consider if broader question (where did he live?) is sufficient

### Assuming the Answer

**Problem**: "Prove that John's father was William"
**Issue**: Biases research toward confirming assumption
**Fix**: Ask "Who was John's father?" and follow evidence

### Skipping the Question

**Problem**: Diving into records without a question
**Issue**: No direction, no way to evaluate success
**Fix**: Always start with a specific question

## Integration with Research Logs

Document for each question:
- The question as originally stated
- Refinements made
- Sources consulted
- Findings (positive and negative)
- Current status (answered, in progress, blocked)
- Date last worked on
