# Match Analysis

Basic concepts for evaluating DNA matches and estimating relationships.

## Understanding Shared DNA

### Centimorgans (cM)

The standard unit for measuring shared DNA is the **centimorgan (cM)**.

- Measures genetic length, not physical length
- Total human genome is approximately 6800 cM
- You share more cM with closer relatives

### Shared cM vs. Percentage

| Shared cM | Approximate % |
|-----------|---------------|
| 3400 cM | 50% |
| 1700 cM | 25% |
| 850 cM | 12.5% |
| 425 cM | 6.25% |
| 212 cM | 3.1% |

**Note**: These are averages; actual amounts vary.

### The Overlap Problem

Many relationships share similar cM ranges:
- 1700 cM could be grandparent, half-sibling, or aunt/uncle
- 400 cM could be 1C1R, half 1C, or great-aunt

**You cannot determine exact relationship from cM alone.**

## Finding Common Ancestors

### The Basic Question

> "How are we related?"

To answer, you need to identify the **Most Recent Common Ancestor (MRCA)**.

### Methods

**Shared Matches**
- Look at matches you share with your DNA match
- Shared matches suggest shared ancestry
- Cluster shared matches to identify family groups

**Family Trees**
- Compare your tree to your match's tree
- Look for overlapping surnames and locations
- Common ancestors appear in both trees

**Triangulation**
- Find three or more people who share DNA
- All descend from common ancestor
- Helps confirm specific ancestral connection

### When You Find the Connection

Once you identify the common ancestor:
1. Count generations from you to MRCA
2. Count generations from match to MRCA
3. This determines the relationship

**Cousin Calculation**:
- Same generation = "Xth cousins"
- Different generations = "Xth cousins Y times removed"

## Integrating DNA with Documentary Research

### DNA as One Piece of Evidence

DNA evidence should be combined with:
- Documentary records (vital, census, etc.)
- Known family history
- Other relatives' DNA results

### Confirming Documented Relationships

Use DNA to verify paper trail:
1. Document relationship traditionally
2. Predict expected shared DNA
3. Compare actual DNA match
4. If consistent, relationship confirmed

### Breaking Brick Walls

When documents are lacking:
1. Build out known family thoroughly
2. Test multiple family members
3. Analyze match patterns
4. Form hypotheses from DNA patterns
5. Seek documentary confirmation

## Common Match Patterns

### Close Family Pattern

High cM + clear family connection = straightforward

**Example**: Match shares 1750 cM, tree shows common grandparents → Half-sibling or aunt/uncle confirmed.

### Endogamy Pattern

Multiple relationships through intermarried communities.

**Signs**:
- Higher than expected cM for relationship
- Many shared matches in same community
- Ancestors from endogamous population (Ashkenazi Jewish, colonial New England, etc.)

### Half Relationship Pattern

Lower than expected for full relationship.

**Example**: Expected 1st cousin (~866 cM) but share ~400 cM → Possibly half-1st cousin (share one grandparent, not two).

### No Shared DNA

Expected match but no shared DNA.

**Possible causes**:
- Relationship is too distant (random inheritance)
- One person is not biologically related as believed
- Different ancestral lines than assumed

## Next Steps After Finding Match

1. **Document the match**
   - Shared cM
   - Shared segments (if available)
   - Shared matches

2. **Review their tree**
   - Look for surnames
   - Note locations
   - Identify potential connections

3. **Compare to your tree**
   - Look for overlaps
   - Note common geography

4. **Reach out if appropriate**
   - Respectful contact
   - Share what you know
   - Ask about their research

5. **Document conclusions**
   - Record the relationship
   - Cite the DNA evidence
   - Connect to documentary evidence
