# Proof Arguments

GPS Element 5: Writing sound, coherent conclusions.

## The Standard

A genealogical conclusion must be:
- **Sound**: Based on logical reasoning from evidence
- **Written**: Documented in prose form
- **Coherent**: Understandable by others
- **Complete**: Addresses all evidence

## Types of Proof Writing

### Proof Summary

A brief statement adequate for simple cases.

**Use when**:
- Evidence is straightforward
- Sources agree
- No significant conflicts
- Standard genealogical situation

**Format**: Brief paragraph stating conclusion with key sources.

### Proof Argument

Extended analysis for complex cases.

**Use when**:
- Evidence is indirect or negative
- Sources conflict
- Identity is confused
- Relationship requires reasoning

**Format**: Multi-paragraph analysis walking through evidence and reasoning.

### Case Study

Comprehensive treatment for difficult problems.

**Use when**:
- Publishing research
- Establishing precedent
- Teaching methodology
- Especially complex situation

**Format**: Article-length with full documentation and analysis.

## Proof Summary Format

### Structure

```
[Conclusion statement]. [Key evidence, cited]. [Supporting
evidence, cited]. [Any limitations or notes].
```

### Example

```
John Smith, who died in Muskingum County, Ohio, in 1925, was
born 15 March 1850 in Zanesville, Ohio. His birth is recorded
in the Ohio birth register.¹ Census records consistently show
his birth year as 1850.² His tombstone confirms the birth
date.³

¹ Ohio, Births, 1850, Muskingum County, vol. 3, p. 45, John Smith.
² 1860-1900 U.S. Census, Muskingum County, Ohio, various pages.
³ Woodlawn Cemetery, Zanesville, Ohio, gravestone of John Smith.
```

## Proof Argument Format

### Structure

1. **Introduction**: State the question and conclusion
2. **Background**: Provide necessary context
3. **Evidence**: Present each piece systematically
4. **Analysis**: Explain how evidence connects
5. **Resolution**: Address conflicts if any
6. **Conclusion**: Restate finding with confidence level

### Example Structure

```
INTRODUCTION
The parentage of Mary Jones (1825-1900) of Muskingum County,
Ohio, has been unclear due to lack of vital records from
the early 19th century. This analysis establishes that she
was the daughter of William Jones and Elizabeth Brown.

BACKGROUND
Mary Jones first appears in records in 1845 when she married
Thomas Smith in Muskingum County... [Context about family,
location, time period]

EVIDENCE
Three sources provide information about Mary's parentage:

Source 1: [Full analysis of first source]
Source 2: [Full analysis of second source]
Source 3: [Full analysis of third source]

ANALYSIS
When compared, these sources reveal... [How evidence connects,
what patterns emerge, what can be inferred]

RESOLUTION OF CONFLICTS
The 1880 census names Mary's father as "James Jones" rather
than William. This discrepancy is explained by... [Address
each conflict]

CONCLUSION
Based on the preponderance of evidence, Mary Jones was the
daughter of William Jones and Elizabeth Brown. This conclusion
rests primarily on... [Summary of key evidence]

Remaining uncertainty: [Any qualifications]
```

## Writing Effective Conclusions

### Be Specific

**Weak**: "John was probably born around 1850."
**Strong**: "John was born 15 March 1850 in Zanesville, Ohio."

### State Confidence

**Unclear**: "John's parents were William and Mary."
**Clear**: "The evidence strongly supports that John's parents were William and Mary Smith."

### Connect to Evidence

**Unsupported**: "John died in 1925."
**Supported**: "John died 15 October 1925, as recorded in his death certificate."

### Address Alternatives

**Incomplete**: "John was the son of William Smith."
**Complete**: "John was the son of William Smith, not the William Smith of neighboring Franklin County who died in 1890."

## Common Writing Patterns

### Establishing Identity

```
[Name] can be identified as the same person across records based on:
- Consistent name
- Matching age/birth year
- Same location
- Continuous presence
- Matching family members
```

### Establishing Parentage

```
[Child name] was the child of [Father] and [Mother] based on:
- Birth/baptism record naming parents
- Census showing child in parents' household
- Parent's will/probate naming child
- DNA evidence (if applicable)
```

### Establishing Marriage

```
[Spouse 1] and [Spouse 2] were married based on:
- Marriage record dated [date]
- Subsequent census showing married couple
- Children born to the union
- Identified as spouse in death/probate records
```

### Using Negative Evidence

```
[Name] is not found in [expected record] despite a thorough
search. This absence, combined with [positive evidence],
suggests that [conclusion].
```

## Quality Checklist

### Before Finalizing, Verify:

**Content**:
- [ ] Conclusion clearly stated
- [ ] All evidence presented
- [ ] Sources properly cited
- [ ] Conflicts addressed
- [ ] Reasoning explained

**Logic**:
- [ ] Conclusion follows from evidence
- [ ] No logical leaps
- [ ] Alternative explanations considered
- [ ] Confidence level appropriate

**Clarity**:
- [ ] Readable by non-expert
- [ ] Organized logically
- [ ] Technical terms explained
- [ ] Flow is coherent

**Documentation**:
- [ ] All sources cited
- [ ] Citations complete
- [ ] Evidence linked to sources
- [ ] Analysis documented

## Avoiding Common Mistakes

### Circular Reasoning

**Problem**: Using conclusion to prove conclusion.
**Example**: "John was born in 1850 because his age at death confirms he was born in 1850."
**Fix**: Show independent evidence supporting conclusion.

### Unsupported Leaps

**Problem**: Jumping to conclusions without showing evidence.
**Example**: "Mary was obviously the daughter of William." (No evidence cited)
**Fix**: Cite specific evidence for each claim.

### Ignoring Conflicts

**Problem**: Presenting only supporting evidence.
**Example**: Omitting census showing different birthplace.
**Fix**: Address all evidence, especially conflicting.

### Overconfidence

**Problem**: Stating as certain what is only probable.
**Example**: "John was definitely..." (when evidence is circumstantial)
**Fix**: Match confidence language to evidence strength.

### Underconfidence

**Problem**: Hedging when evidence is strong.
**Example**: "John might possibly have been..." (when evidence is clear)
**Fix**: State confidently when evidence supports.

## Templates

### Simple Proof Summary

```
[Person name] was the [relationship] of [related person(s)].
This is established by [primary evidence], [supporting
evidence], and [additional support]. [Any qualifications].
```

### Complex Proof Argument Outline

```
I. Question and Conclusion
   - What is being proven
   - Summary of conclusion

II. Historical Context
   - Time and place
   - Available records
   - Family background

III. Evidence Analysis
   A. Source 1
      - Citation
      - What it says
      - Classification
      - Significance
   B. Source 2 [etc.]

IV. Correlation of Evidence
   - How sources connect
   - Patterns identified
   - Cumulative picture

V. Addressing Conflicts
   - Conflict 1: Explanation
   - Conflict 2: Explanation

VI. Conclusion
   - Statement of proof
   - Confidence level
   - Limitations acknowledged

VII. Sources (if not footnoted)
```

## Final Review

### The GPS Check

Before concluding, verify all five elements:

1. **Exhaustive research**: Have I searched all likely sources?
2. **Complete citations**: Can others verify my sources?
3. **Thorough analysis**: Have I evaluated all evidence?
4. **Resolved conflicts**: Have I explained contradictions?
5. **Sound conclusion**: Is my reasoning clear and logical?

If any element is missing, the proof is incomplete.
